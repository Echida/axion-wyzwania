# Note: What is an amplitude, and how does it become a measurement result?

## What confused me

When I first read the lecture, I thought the amplitude was the **length** of the qubit's arrow (I mixed it up with the angle θ on the Bloch sphere). Later I understood that the length of the Bloch arrow is always 1, and the amplitudes decide its **direction**, not its length.

## What an amplitude is

A qubit state is written as

    |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

α and β are the amplitudes: simply the numbers in front of |0⟩ and |1⟩ (in code, the two entries of the state vector).

An amplitude is **not** a probability itself. The probability is its **square** (Born rule):

    P(0) = |α|²      P(1) = |β|²      and |α|² + |β|² = 1

Example: |ψ⟩ = 0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩ gives P(0) = 0.36 and P(1) = 0.64.

Why not just use probabilities? Because amplitudes can be negative (or complex) and probabilities cannot. |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2 give the same 50/50 probabilities, but they are different states: on the Bloch sphere one points to +x and the other to −x. Only the sign of β is different.

## Amplitudes and the Bloch sphere

On the Bloch sphere, α = cos(θ/2) and β = e^{iφ} sin(θ/2), where θ is measured from the top (|0⟩).

| arrow position     | θ    | α     | β     | P(0) | P(1) |
|--------------------|------|-------|-------|------|------|
| top (\|0⟩)          | 0°   | 1     | 0     | 1    | 0    |
| equator (\|+⟩)      | 90°  | 1/√2  | 1/√2  | 1/2  | 1/2  |
| bottom (\|1⟩)       | 180° | 0     | 1     | 0    | 1    |

On the equator the probabilities must be 1/2 each, so the amplitudes must be √(1/2) = 1/√2. The formula gives the same: cos(90°/2) = cos 45° = 1/√2.

## From probabilities to measurement results (in my simulator)

The probabilities are exact, but a single measurement result is random. My function `measure(state, shots)` works like this:

1. compute the probabilities |amplitude|² for every basis state (00, 01, 10, 11),
2. "roll a weighted die" `shots` times with `np.random.choice`; each roll picks **one** label,
3. count how many times each label was picked.

For the Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 I got `{'00': 474, '01': 0, '10': 0, '11': 526}`. 01 and 10 never appear because their amplitudes are 0. 00 and 11 are not exactly 500 each because the rolls are random, like flipping a coin 1000 times. With more shots, the **fraction** of each result gets closer to its probability (law of large numbers).

Note: the randomness in numpy is pseudo-random (generated by an algorithm from a seed). This is fine for a simulator, because only the statistics have to match a real quantum computer.
